Reference · sources & scoring

Where every number comes from and how it's scored

How the overall score works. Every column is converted to a 1–10 rating where higher is always better for someone arriving with little. The overall score is the weighted average: score = Σ(rating × weight) ÷ Σ(weights). Weights don't need to total 100; they're normalized.

To edit this file. Each city's ratings live in the CITIES array as s:[...], indexed [0 upside, 1 market, 2 entry, 3 approve, 4 rent, 5 tax, 6 climate, 7 lifestyle, 8 crime, 9 homePrice]. Dollar and rate values are separate fields (rent, home, crime, dem). Default weights are in FACTORS[].def.

Entry-level jobs 13% rated s[2]
Source: BLS Local Area Unemployment Statistics (metro unemployment rate) plus volume of retail/food/warehouse postings on Indeed or Lightcast. Lower unemployment + high posting volume = easier to get hired fast.
Bands: 9–10 = unemployment ≤3.5% with heavy service/logistics hiring · 7–8 = 3.5–4.5%, steady hiring · 5–6 = 4.5–5.5% · 3–4 = ≥5.5% or a saturated entry market

Bands: 10 = very large, growing degree-level market · 8 = large and stable · 6 = mid-size, modest growth · 4 = thin or shrinking
Economic upside 15% rated s[0]
Source: a 60/40 blend. Existing ceiling (60%): Fortune 500 HQ count by metro and BLS OES 90th-percentile wage for management and professional occupations. Business dynamism (40%): whether companies are arriving or leaving: CBRE headquarters-relocation tracking (725 HQ moves nationally 2018–2025), Census Business Formation Statistics for new-business applications, Census/BLS business dynamics for establishment births vs. deaths, Tax Foundation State Business Tax Climate Index, and Site Selection Magazine's corporate facility-project counts.
Ceiling bands: 10 = ≥8 major HQs or 90th-pct wage ≥$180K · 8–9 = 4–7 HQs / ~$150K · 6–7 = 1–3 HQs / ~$130K · ≤5 = no major HQs / ≤$115K.
Dynamism bands: 10 = strong net HQ inflow and high business formation (Dallas-Fort Worth gained 111 relocations 2018–2025, Austin 88) · 7–9 = net gainer (FL, NC, TN, AZ, GA, CO) · 5–6 = mixed, losing HQs but still winning facility projects (Chicago) · ≤4 = sustained net outflow (Bay Area −156 HQs 2018–2024; NYC metro second-highest departures).

Source: RentCafe, Zumper National Rent Report, Apartment List, metro average one-bedroom rent, mid-2026. 1BR is the scored figure because studios are scarce outside dense pre-war cores; most Sun Belt inventory is 1BR/2BR garden-style. The studio average is shown beneath each 1BR figure as unscored reference.
Bands (1BR): 10 = ≤$1,150 · 9 = $1,300 · 8 = $1,400 · 7 = $1,500 · 6 = $1,650 · 5 = $1,800 · 4 = $2,000 · 3 = $2,300 · 2 = $2,700 · 1 = ≥$3,200
Lease approval 5.7% rated s[3]
Source: Census Housing Vacancy Survey and CoStar/Apartment List vacancy + concession rates. Test: does a full-time entry wage (~$16–20/hr) clear the standard 3×-rent rule against local studio rent?
Bands: 9–10 = vacancy ≥8%, widespread concessions, entry wage clears 3× · 6–8 = balanced market, clears with a co-signer or extra deposit · 3–5 = tight market, entry wage falls short · 1–2 = ≤4% vacancy, broker fees, strict screening

Source: Zillow Home Value Index, Redfin Data Center, NAR metro median sale prices.
Bands: 10 = ≤$300K · 9 = $325K · 8 = $350K · 7 = $400K · 6 = $450K · 5 = $500K · 4 = $575K · 3 = $700K · 2 = $950K · 1 = ≥$1.2M
Price-to-Income Ratio 1% measured s[10]
Source: median home price ÷ median household income, both metro-level, from Zillow ZHVI or Redfin over Census ACS Table B19013. This is the standard "median multiple" housing-affordability measure (Demographia / Harvard JCHS use the same construction). It asks whether a typical household on a typical income can buy a typical home, not whether a high earner can.
Bands (ratio): 10 = ≤2.5× · 9 = 3.0× · 8 = 3.5× · 7 = 4.0× · 6 = 4.5× · 5 = 5.0× · 4 = 5.5× · 3 = 6.5× · 2 = 8.0× · 1 = >8.0×
Convention: under 3.0× is broadly considered affordable, 3–4× moderately unaffordable, 4–5× seriously unaffordable, over 5× severely unaffordable.
Lifestyle 15% rated s[7]
Source: a 60/40 blend of two things. Social scene (60%): venues and restaurants per capita, share of residents aged 25–34 (Census ACS), net in-migration of young adults. Natural amenities (40%): access to beaches, lakes, hiking, mountains and scenery, benchmarked against the USDA ERS Natural Amenities Scale and drive time to coast, water and trailheads.
Scene bands: 10 = major cultural hub · 8–9 = strong scene, young and growing · 5–6 = decent but quiet · ≤4 = limited or commuter-suburb feel.
Nature bands: 10 = beaches and mountains both within reach (LA, Denver) · 7–8 = one outstanding asset nearby (Gulf beaches, Blue Ridge) · 5 = modest, a couple of hours to anything · ≤3 = flat and landlocked.
Tax Savings 4% measured s[5]
Source: Tax Foundation State Individual Income Tax Rates; state revenue departments. Include local earnings taxes (e.g. Kansas City's 1%).
Bands: 10 = no state income tax (FL, TX, TN) · 8 = flat ≤3% · 6 = 4–5.5% top rate · 4 = 5.5–7%, or lower rate plus local earnings tax · 3 = ~5% plus heavy local/property burden · 2 = progressive top rate ≥6.5% (CA, NY, NJ)
Crime 9.7% measured s[8]
Source: FBI Crime Data Explorer (cde.ucr.cjis.gov), agency/metro NIBRS. Use total index crime = violent + property per 100,000, not violent alone. This column is inverted, like Climate Risk: a higher crime rate produces a higher number, so 10 = worst. It is subtracted in the overall score, not added. Cross-check with local PD annual reports; NIBRS coverage is uneven and some agencies under-report.
Bands (higher crime rate = higher score), total per 100k: 1 = ≤1,500 · 2 = 1,800 · 3 = 2,300 · 4 = 2,600 · 5 = 3,000 · 6 = 3,600 · 7 = 4,300 · 8 = 5,200 · 9 = 6,200 · 10 = ≥7,000
Note: the table displays the 1–10 rating; the underlying rate per 100k is kept in each city's crime field for reference. Property crime dominates the total, so cities with low violence but heavy theft (San Francisco) rate low here, which is intended.
Climate Risk 5.4% rated s[6]
Source: FEMA National Risk Index expected annual loss by county; NOAA billion-dollar disaster records; state insurance-department nonrenewal rates and average homeowner premiums; NOAA climate normals for heat and cold mortality; Colorado River and Great Lakes water reporting. Measures risk to property and person, not comfort. Insurance pricing is the sharpest available signal: Florida homeowners now pay roughly $7,100–$15,000 a year against a $2,500–$3,500 national average, Florida nonrenewals reached ~3% statewide and parts of California ~8%.
Formula: 0.50 × property-damage risk + 0.30 × personal danger + 0.20 × long-term vulnerability, rounded. This column is inverted: 10 = worst risk. All three sub-scores point the same way, so a higher number is always more exposure. It is subtracted in the overall score, not added.
Property damage (1–10): hurricane, surge, flood, wildfire, tornado, hail and quake exposure, weighted by insurance cost and availability. 10 = severe hazard with insurers withdrawing · 1 = low hazard, normal premiums.
Personal danger (1–10): deaths and injury from extreme heat, extreme cold and storms. Phoenix rates high here despite moderate property risk; Maricopa County records hundreds of heat deaths annually.
Long-term vulnerability (1–10): water insecurity, sea-level rise and worsening hazard trend. Stated as vulnerability rather than resilience so it runs the same direction as the other two.
Age groups reference only reference age[]
Source: U.S. Census Bureau ACS Table B01001 (Sex by Age), metro/MSA level. Shown as share of total population aged 18–34, 35–64 and 65+; under-18 is omitted, so the three columns don't total 100%.
Not scored. Useful as a peer-group and life-stage signal. A high 18–34 share means more people your age; a high 65+ share signals a retirement-skewed metro.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau ACS Table B19013 (Median Household Income in the Past 12 Months), metro/MSA level.
Not scored. Read it against Studio rent, not on its own. A high median income often means a high cost floor, and it describes established households, not what you'd earn on arrival.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau ACS Table B03002 (Hispanic or Latino Origin by Race), metro/MSA level; 2020 Census for baseline.

Primary sources. FBI Crime Data Explorer (cde.ucr.cjis.gov); U.S. Census Bureau (2020 Census, ACS B03002, Housing Vacancy Survey); U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (OES wages, CES metro employment, LAUS unemployment); Zillow ZHVI and Redfin Data Center; RentCafe, Zumper, Apartment List; Tax Foundation and state revenue departments; NOAA climate normals; FEMA National Risk Index; USDA cropland data; Fortune 500 HQ listings; Lightcast job-posting data.

Honesty note. Dollar figures, tax rates and demographics come from the datasets above. The 1–10 ratings are calibrated to the bands listed but were assigned by judgment, not computed row-by-row from a live dataset, so any single rating can be wrong. The bands are published here precisely so a rating can be checked against the source and corrected. Where a number conflicts with what you've seen on the ground, trust your eyes and change it.
